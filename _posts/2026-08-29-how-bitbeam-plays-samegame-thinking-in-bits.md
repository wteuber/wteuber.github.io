SameGame looks simple. You get a grid of coloured tiles. Click any group of two or more touching tiles of the same colour and the group vanishes. Whatever sat above it falls down, empty columns close up, and large groups score far better than small ones. There are two things you might be playing for, and they are not the same thing: the highest score, or an empty board. Clearing usually pays a bonus, but the highest-scoring game is often not the one that finishes the board.

The difficulty shows up within a minute or two. Every click rearranges the board, so the group you were saving for later might not exist by the time you get to it. Judging a move exactly means playing out everything that follows from it, which nobody can afford. Judging it cheaply means guessing. How good that guess is turns out to be the whole game.

I wrote a solver for this called bitbeam. It clears a 30×20 three-colour board in two to four seconds, and a 15×10 one in a fifth of a second or less. Three ideas do most of that work, and two further things turned out to matter that I had not expected to.

Every number in this post was measured on the machine I wrote it on: an Intel i9-9880H, 8 physical cores and 16 hardware threads, Apple clang with `-O3 -march=native`. Each one names the board it was measured on, because the interesting sizes differ from section to section.

## Why you cannot just try every move

On a 30×20 board, a typical position offers a few dozen legal clicks, and a full game runs about fifty clicks deep. Multiply that out and you get a number with more than a hundred digits. The observable universe contains fewer atoms.

So checking every possibility is off the table permanently, on any hardware anyone is going to build. The useful question is not how to search everything. It is what to throw away, and how cheaply you can throw it away.

## Idea 1: Keep a shortlist

The technique is called [beam search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_search), and it is about as simple as search algorithms get.

Take the current position and play every legal click. Score all the resulting positions, keep the best 5,000, delete the rest. Now do the same thing starting from those 5,000. Repeat until the games are over. Five thousand is the width bitbeam plays at when the bot drives it; the benchmarks further down use wider beams, and each says which.

It is a pruned form of best-first search, which is the more familiar approach of ranking every partial solution you have generated by some heuristic and always extending the most promising one. Best-first has to hold on to all of them, so its memory grows as the search runs. Beam search caps that set at a fixed size, which turns memory from something the search decides into a number you pick before it starts. In return it is greedy. It always finishes and reports a game, but that game need not be the best one, and on a board that can be cleared it can easily fail to find the clear.

The beam is the size of that shortlist. Widen it and the solver plays better and runs slower. Narrow it and it plays worse and runs faster. This is roughly how a strong human plays: look at the moves that seem promising and ignore the ones that obviously are not.

The hard part is the scoring. Ranking positions by points scored so far works badly, because the solver grabs cheap points early and paints itself into a corner. What works much better is points scored so far plus the points still sitting on the board, meaning the score you would collect from every group currently on it if you could take them all as they stand. And if the goal is an empty board rather than a high score, the ranking also has to charge a penalty per tile that sits in no clickable group at all, because those are exactly the tiles standing between the position and a clear. Without that term the search will happily trade the clean finish away for one fat group.

## Idea 2: Shrink the board until gravity is one instruction

The obvious way to store the board is one number per tile in a grid. Removing a group then means a recursive flood fill to find the connected tiles, and applying gravity means a nested loop that shuffles tiles downward one at a time.

bitbeam stores each column as three 32-bit integers instead. Every tile gets three bits, one in each of the three integers, which is enough to encode eight colours. A fourth number records how tall the column is. Tiles rest on the floor of their column by the rules of the game, so that height is also the occupancy: the bottom `colh[x]` bits are tiles and everything above is empty, which is why an empty cell needs no colour of its own. That is the entire board:

```cpp
using Bits = uint32_t;

struct Board {
  uint8_t w;                          // columns still standing
  uint8_t colh[MAXW];                 // how tall each column is
  Bits p0[MAXW], p1[MAXW], p2[MAXW];  // three colour bitplanes per column

  Bits occ(int x) const {             // which cells hold a tile: the low colh bits
    return static_cast<Bits>((uint64_t{1} << colh[x]) - 1);
  }

  int colorAt(int x, int y) const {   // read one tile back out
    return ((p0[x] >> y) & 1)
         | (((p1[x] >> y) & 1) << 1)
         | (((p2[x] >> y) & 1) << 2);
  }
};
```

A 30-column board is 90 integers and 30 heights, a few hundred bytes in total, and each of those integers is a single machine word the CPU can operate on in one register. That last part is what makes it fast, because of one instruction.

[PEXT](https://www.felixcloutier.com/x86/pext), short for parallel bit extract, takes a value and a mask of the bits you want to keep, then packs those bits down to the bottom in order. It does this in a single instruction. That operation is not a metaphor for gravity within a column. It is gravity, so removing a group and letting everything fall is not a loop over tiles at all:

```cpp
// mask[x] is the clicked group's tiles in column x; lo and hi are the leftmost
// and rightmost columns it reaches. pext and popcnt are the CPU instructions.
for (int x = lo; x <= hi; ++x) {   // only the columns the group touches
  if (!mask[x]) continue;
  Bits keep = b.occ(x) & ~mask[x]; // the tiles that survive this click
  b.p0[x] = pext(b.p0[x], keep);   // each plane drops to the column floor,
  b.p1[x] = pext(b.p1[x], keep);   // original order preserved,
  b.p2[x] = pext(b.p2[x], keep);   // one instruction per plane
  b.colh[x] = popcnt(keep);        // new column height, from a bit count
}
```

Three instructions and a population count, on the two or three columns the group actually spans, instead of a nested loop over the board. The game's other rule, that an emptied column closes up and the columns to its right slide over, is a separate step, but a cheap and rare one: it moves four integers per column shifted and only fires when a column runs out of tiles entirely.

PEXT is not free of strings. It is x86-only, present since Haswell in 2013, and on AMD parts before Zen 3 it was microcoded and slow enough to lose to plain shifting. bitbeam carries a portable bit-by-bit fallback for that reason, checked against the instruction on 100,000 random cases so the two paths are known to agree.

Finding groups gets similar treatment. Instead of walking tile by tile, each column is broken into runs of a single colour, and neighbouring columns are compared by XOR-ing their bit patterns together. A set bit in the result means two runs touch and match. The loop then runs once per boundary between groups rather than once per tile, which took group linking on a 15×15 board from 3,450 nanoseconds to 605.

[sgbust](https://github.com/chausner/sgbust) is the solver I used as a reference point. It has the same shape, beam search over a hash set, and stores the board as one byte per cell. It builds through vcpkg and parallelises with `std::execution::par`, which the standard library on my machine does not implement. That is not unfixable — linking TBB is the usual answer, and dropping to `seq` is another — but either way the end-to-end number would be measuring its allocator and its build against mine as much as its algorithm, so I did not go that route. What I did instead was transcribe its three core routines into bitbeam's benchmark, where both versions run on the same 20×20 five-colour board, in the same process, interleaved, single-threaded, best of four rounds:

| | bitbeam | sgbust | speedup |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Enumerate groups | 5,175 ns | 18,410 ns | 3.6× |
| Expand one child | 100 ns | 569 ns | 5.7× |
| Play a game to the end | 248 µs | 1,008 µs | 4.1× |

From 10×10 up to 20×20, and three to seven colours, the pattern holds: 3.2 to 4.0 times on group enumeration, 4.8 to 5.8 times per child, 3.6 to 4.1 times on a whole game. Two caveats. sgbust links the mimalloc allocator and this transcription of it does not, which handicaps its allocation-heavy paths, and these are the per-node primitives rather than the two solvers end to end.

The representation is not a universal win, either. I tried the same trick in a Ruby version of the solver and it ran 2.65 times slower than plain arrays. Ruby has no `Integer#popcount` and no way to reach PEXT, so the two operations that make the C++ version fast are gone, and what is left is an interpreted method call per shift and per mask against C-implemented array indexing. The idea pays off when it maps onto instructions the processor actually has, and not otherwise.

## Idea 3: Do not record what you are about to delete

This was the biggest single end-to-end speedup, and it has nothing to do with bits.

The natural way to write beam search is to produce every child position in full. Copy the board, remove the group, check whether the game is over, serialise the result, hash it, insert it into a set to catch duplicates. Then sort all of them and throw away 95 percent.

Nearly all of that work goes into positions that get deleted moments later.

Every candidate still has to be built and scored — that is how you learn what it is worth. What bitbeam does not do is keep it. The first pass writes down six bytes per candidate and nothing else:

```cpp
w.objs.push_back(objective(cscore, w.child, w.cmg)); // 4 bytes: how good
w.mvs.push_back(static_cast<uint16_t>(g));           // 2 bytes: which move
```

No board is copied into storage, nothing is serialised, nothing is hashed, nothing goes into the duplicate set. The solver finds the exact cut-off from those six-byte stubs, then makes a second pass that replays only the moves above it and stores those. Replaying a move is a handful of PEXTs; the expensive parts — serialising, hashing, the dedup insert — now happen only for survivors, and a parent is unpacked at most once however many of its children got through. The search is the same and the output is the same, byte for byte. On a 15×15 board at a beam of 10,000, runtime went from 3,718 milliseconds to 1,306.

Sorting cheap descriptions and keeping only the winners beats materialising everything and then sorting.

## Getting the same answer twice

Run a parallel search twice and you will usually get two different answers. Neither is wrong. They differ because the boundary between "good enough to keep" and "cut" landed in a slightly different place depending on which thread happened to finish first. That makes bugs hard to reproduce, and it makes the question "did my change help?" unanswerable.

Fixing it meant defining a tiebreak for every comparison in the search, so that no ordering is ever left to whichever thread got there first. The comparison that decides who makes the shortlist is the important one:

```cpp
auto better = [](const uint8_t* a, const uint8_t* b) {
  float oa = getObj(a), ob = getObj(b);
  if (oa != ob) return oa > ob;   // best objective wins
  return getHash(a) < getHash(b); // tie: the board itself decides
};
```

The same treatment applies further down. If two different move sequences reach the same position with the same objective, keep the lexicographically smaller sequence. If two finished games score the same, prefer the shorter one, then again the lexicographically smaller one.

The result is that the output is byte-identical whether the solver runs on 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 threads, which the test suite checks on random boards. It did not cost the parallel speedup: on a 15×15 board at a beam of 20,000, sixteen threads still finish 5.7 times faster than one, on eight physical cores with the last factor coming from hyperthreads. That is a claim about scaling, not a zero-cost proof — I never built a non-deterministic version to race it against at equal thread count, so what I can say is that the ordering guarantees left the scaling intact, not that they are free.

## Knowing when a board cannot be cleared

Deciding whether a SameGame board can be cleared at all is NP-complete, and it stays hard at surprisingly small sizes. [Two colours and two columns is already enough](https://erikdemaine.org/papers/Clickomania_MOVES2015/). Some boards simply cannot be cleared, and a beam search hunting for a clear on one of those will not hang — every click removes tiles, so every game ends — but it will spend its entire budget on the search before reporting the failure, and an exact prover with no way to recognise a dead position can run far longer than that.

bitbeam carries a small set of refutations, which are rules that can prove a board is impossible but can never claim a board is fine.

- A colour with exactly one tile left can never be removed, so that board is dead.
- A single column is clearable if and only if its colour string is generated by the grammar `S → Λ | S S | c S c | c S c S c`, which is [Biedl, Demaine, Demaine, Fleischer, Jacobsen and Munro](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0107031). This one fires constantly, because column collapse drives boards narrow near the end of a game.
- [Takes and Kosters](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~takesfw/pdf/samegame.pdf), Theorem 3: a two-colour board at least five wide and five tall, whose strict lower half is a chessboard, cannot be cleared whatever sits above it. The size condition is doing real work — drop it and the rule is false, because on a narrow board a tile falling onto the chessboard can pair with what it lands on and break the pattern open.

Every rule is checked against exhaustive search on thousands of small boards. A rule that wrongly refutes a solvable board is worse than no rule at all.

They are also worth less than they sound. In the exact prover they cut proofs by 1.0 to 4.4 times in nodes on 6×6 to 8×8 boards, which is where they matter. In beam search on solvable boards they cost about 8 percent and save nearly nothing, which is the expected result: a refutation only pays when the answer is no, and random boards are overwhelmingly solvable. They stay on because the boards where they help are the ones you otherwise never finish.

## Does it play better?

Against the [genetic algorithm](https://github.com/wteuber/samegame_autoplay/tree/master/software/solver/evolutionary) I had been using, on six random 15×10 three-colour boards, with both asked to clear the board completely, and both invoked exactly the way the bot invokes them — the GA at a population of 10 over 200 generations, bitbeam at a beam of 5,000:

| Solver | Mean score | Boards cleared | Mean time |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Genetic algorithm | 1,993 | 6/6 | 775 ms |
| bitbeam | 3,295 | 6/6 | 205 ms |

Two thirds more points in a quarter of the time. The time column is the softer half of that: the genetic algorithm is Ruby on a single core and bitbeam is C++ across all sixteen hardware threads, so it compares the two programs as they actually get used rather than the two algorithms. A genetic algorithm will also buy score with time if you give it more generations. The score column is the one I would defend, and it is not close.

sgbust is absent from this table, and not because it lost. For the build reasons above I have no end-to-end number for it that would be about its algorithm rather than its dependencies. The per-routine comparison earlier is the substitute.

## Wrapping up

None of the ideas here is novel. Beam search has been around for decades, PEXT has been in Intel processors since 2013, and not recording things you are about to throw away is ordinary engineering advice. What mattered was applying them to the same problem and measuring after each step, including the step in Ruby that made things slower.

The determinism work paid off in a way I had not planned for. Once the output stopped varying between runs, evaluating every other change became straightforward, because any difference in the result had to have been caused by the change. The refutations went the other way: sound, satisfying to implement, and worth almost nothing on the boards you actually meet. Both were worth knowing, and neither was on the list when I started.

*The bot in the recording above is a separate program. It reads the board by looking at pixels on the screen and plays by moving the actual mouse, which is a story for another post.*
